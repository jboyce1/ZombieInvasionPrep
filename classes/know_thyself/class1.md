# Skill: Resection

### `0945–1010`

Resection is a land navigation skill used to determine your location by taking bearings to known points and drawing those bearings back onto a map.

Today you will practice using:

* play maps
* grid paper with items
* compasses
* rulers

The basic idea:

* identify two or more known points
* take a bearing to each known point
* draw the back azimuth from each known point onto the map
* your location is near the place where the lines intersect

# Sacred and Profane

### `1010–1035`

A major reason zombies are scary is that they force us to think about what matters.

A zombie story is not only about monsters.

It is also about what people protect, what people abandon, what people fear losing, and what people are willing to sacrifice.

For this discussion, we will use three levels of sacredness:

* personally sacred
* community sacred
* societally sacred

Something can be sacred at one level and not sacred at another.

Something can also be profane, meaning it is not treated as sacred at any of those levels.

# Sacred and Profane Categories

### `1035–1055`

## Profane

Not sacred at any level.

Examples:

* a generic plastic water bottle
* everyday kitchen utensils like a spoon
* a basic stapler in a classroom

## Personally Sacred

Sacred to the individual, but not necessarily sacred to the community or society.

Examples:

* my grandfather’s tools
* a diary
* the spoon in my desk drawer

## Community Sacred

Sacred to a specific community, but not necessarily sacred to the individual or broader society.

Examples:

* a club or fraternity pin or emblem
* a local sports team’s mascot or jersey
* a community’s specific religious symbol not recognized at a larger societal level

## Societally Sacred

Sacred to broader society, but not necessarily sacred to the individual or their immediate community.

Examples:

* the U.S. flag, for someone who does not feel a personal connection to it
* national monuments or historical sites
* a copy of the U.S. Constitution

## Personally and Community Sacred

Sacred to the individual and their community, but not necessarily to broader society.

Examples:

* a family Bible or religious book
* a family recipe passed down through generations and also valued by a cultural group
* cultural clothing worn at specific community events

## Personally and Societally Sacred

Sacred to the individual and broader society, but not necessarily to the immediate community.

Examples:

* a family member’s military medals
* a charity or social cause that matters personally and is recognized more broadly
* a historical figure or leader the student deeply admires and society also respects

## Community and Societally Sacred

Sacred to a community and broader society, but not deeply personal to the individual.

Examples:

* a national holiday celebrated by the community
* a statue of a historical figure located in the community
* a religious holiday recognized by both community and society

## Personally, Community, and Societally Sacred

Sacred at all three levels.

Examples:

* human life
* freedom of choice
* liberty
* the pursuit of happiness, so long as it does not prevent others from pursuing happiness

# Emergency Ethics Deliberation

### `1055–1115`

In disasters, pandemics, conflicts, and emergencies, people are often forced to make decisions where something sacred must be weighed against something else sacred.

These questions do not always have easy answers.

The goal is not to win the argument.

The goal is to understand what values are in conflict.

As you discuss, think about:

* What is personally sacred?
* What is community sacred?
* What is societally sacred?
* What is being treated as profane?
* Who gets protected?
* Who gets sacrificed?
* Who decides?
* What happens when the group disagrees?
* What does the decision reveal about what the group values?

---

# Deliberation Questions

## 1. The Infected Family Member

A person in your survival group has been bitten. They are still awake, still talking, and still clearly themselves.

Their family says they are sacred and must be protected.

The group says everyone else’s survival is sacred too.

What should happen?

## 2. The Limited Medicine

There is only enough medicine for one person.

One person is a child.

One person is the group’s doctor.

One person is your closest friend.

Who gets the medicine?

What value is your decision protecting?

## 3. The Quarantine Zone

A neighborhood may be infected.

Officials want to block all exits for 72 hours.

Some people inside may be healthy.

Some people outside may be at risk if the quarantine fails.

When is it acceptable to restrict the freedom of innocent people?

## 4. The Bridge

A bridge is the only way out of the city.

A large crowd is trying to cross.

If everyone crosses at once, the bridge may collapse.

If the group waits too long, the zombies arrive.

Who controls the bridge?

Who goes first?

## 5. The Loud Baby

A group is hiding in a building.

A baby will not stop crying.

The sound may attract zombies.

The baby is sacred to the family.

The group’s safety is sacred to everyone else.

What obligations does the group have?

What obligations does the family have?

## 6. The Abandoned Stranger

A stranger is trapped nearby and asking for help.

Helping them may put the group at risk.

Ignoring them may mean leaving an innocent person to die.

Does the group owe the stranger anything?

Does the answer change if the stranger is injured, old, young, infected, useful, or unknown?

## 7. The Useful Bad Person

A person in the group is cruel, selfish, and dangerous to group trust.

They are also the only person who knows how to repair the water system.

Is usefulness enough to protect someone’s place in the group?

When does a person become too dangerous to keep?

## 8. The Sacred Object

A student can save either a backpack of food or a personally sacred object from home.

The food helps the group.

The object helps the student keep their identity, memory, and hope.

Which matters more?

Does survival require giving up sacred things?

## 9. The Community Symbol

The group wants to destroy a local monument to use its stone for barricades.

Some people say the monument is sacred to the community.

Others say survival matters more than symbols.

When can a community destroy its own sacred objects?

## 10. The Dangerous Truth

The group leader knows that the situation is worse than everyone thinks.

Telling the truth may cause panic.

Keeping quiet may preserve order.

Does the group have a right to know?

Can lying be protective?

## 11. The Outsider Camp

Another group asks to join your shelter.

Your group has food, water, and walls.

Their group has children and injured people.

Letting them in may weaken your group’s survival chances.

Keeping them out may condemn them.

Where does responsibility end?

## 12. The One Who Is Sacred Somewhere Else

A stranger may not be personally sacred to you.

They may not be part of your community.

But they are sacred to someone.

How should that change your decision?

Does every person have sacred value even when they are not “your people”?

# Private Reflection

### `1115–1120`

Choose one question from the deliberation that was difficult to answer.

Respond privately.

Consider:

* What made the question difficult?
* Which sacred values were in conflict?
* Did your answer protect the individual, the community, or society?
* Did your answer change as other people talked?
* What does your answer reveal about what you value?
* What would be hardest to live with afterward?

Your reflection does not need to be shared.

The goal is to think honestly.
