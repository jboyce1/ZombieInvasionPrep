**Focus**: interpreting topographic terrain and turning a mapped route into a survival narrative

**Can another person reconstruct a journey from the terrain evidence in your story?**

## Objectives

# Skill: Assess a Head Injury

### `0945–1005`

This is a basic first-response assessment, not a diagnosis. Use a simulated scenario during practice; do not strike, shake, or intentionally make another student dizzy.

1. Make sure the scene is safe and ask what happened.
2. Check whether the person is awake, breathing normally, and able to answer simple questions.
3. Ask simple orientation and memory questions, such as their name, location, what happened, and what they remember immediately before and after it.
4. Look for confusion, worsening headache, repeated vomiting, slurred speech, unequal pupils, unusual behavior, weakness, loss of balance, or poor coordination.
5. If a head, neck, or spinal injury is possible, tell the person not to move and keep them in the position found unless the area is unsafe.
6. Get the instructor and activate emergency help for danger signs, loss of consciousness, a seizure, increasing confusion, or a worsening condition. Continue watching for changes.

Do not allow a person with a suspected concussion to return to activity until they have been evaluated by an appropriate healthcare professional.

[CDC HEADS UP: Signs and Symptoms of Concussion](https://www.cdc.gov/heads-up/signs-symptoms/index.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}

# Review: Terrain Features on Topographic Maps

### `1005–1025`

Review how contour lines represent the shape of land. Be prepared to recognize and describe:

* hilltop
* ridge
* saddle
* spur
* draw
* valley
* steep, gradual, and flat ground
* uphill and downhill movement

Use the class handout during the review. The goal is to read the contour evidence—not memorize one example.

# Topo Narrative Assignment

### `1045–1120`

Your group will write a story of survival that follows an actual route on the Pittsburgh West topographic map. The route comes first; the narrative must preserve enough geographic evidence for another group to reconstruct it during the next class.

Read the complete rubric before choosing the route. Your narrative should communicate the required terrain sequence, direction, distance, and changes in the land through the events of the story. Do not draw the answer route into the narrative or identify every coordinate directly—the reader must interpret your evidence.
