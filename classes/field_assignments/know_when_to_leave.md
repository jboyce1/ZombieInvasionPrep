# Asynchronous Mission 2: Know When to Leave

**Your question:** During a mass evacuation, how would your household decide **when** leaving is safer than staying—and how would everyone reach safety if traffic, fuel, communication, and phone navigation fail?

**Your products:**

1. Complete the [editable evacuation-planning workbook (Word)]({{ '/classes/field_assignments/files/know-when-to-leave-student-workbook.docx' | relative_url }}). Type in it with Word or LibreOffice, or print it and write by hand.
2. Turn your conclusions into the [editable evacuation briefing template (PowerPoint)]({{ '/classes/field_assignments/files/know-when-to-leave-presentation-template.pptx' | relative_url }}).

The **workbook** contains your detailed evidence, maps, calculations, and decisions for the teacher. The **presentation** communicates the plan clearly. Do not paste the entire workbook into the slides.

**Time:** about 3–4 hours. You may divide the work across several days.

<div class="warning-box">
<strong>The zombies are the MacGuffin.</strong> The plan should also work for a flood, wildfire, hazardous-material release, infrastructure failure, or another real event. This is a planning exercise. You will not travel, test a route, contact emergency services, or begin a real evacuation for this assignment.

## Situation report

An expanding emergency may soon make your area unsafe. Officials have warned residents to prepare, but they have **not yet ordered your household to evacuate**. Other households are already leaving. If a mass evacuation begins, major roads may gridlock, fuel may be unavailable, public transportation may change, and mobile data may fail.

Your household must decide whether to:

* leave before an order;
* wait for an official evacuation order;
* remain ready to leave when a pre-decided trigger occurs; or
* shelter temporarily because travel is more dangerous.

There is no perfect choice. Leaving too early has costs. Leaving too late can remove every good option. Your job is to replace panic and wishful thinking with researched decision points.

## Safety and privacy boundaries

Complete this mission with an adult’s knowledge. Research from home; do not walk, bicycle, or drive a route for class credit.

Never submit:

* your home address or a map pin on your home;
* names, phone numbers, passwords, alarm details, or hidden keys;
* exact locations of valuables, medications, weapons, fuel, or emergency supplies;
* a private family destination’s address;
* a child’s independent travel plan.

Use a nearby public landmark or general neighborhood as the starting point. Identify people by relationship and destinations by type or general area. A family may keep the exact version privately and submit a redacted version.

During a real emergency, follow evacuation orders and official route instructions. Never enter a closed road, cross floodwater, approach a fire or chemical release, trespass, use railroad tracks, or walk/bicycle on a limited-access highway.

# Phase 1 — Define two different threats

In **Workbook Section 1**, select two plausible hazards that would make you leave in different directions. Examples include:

* river flooding versus a wind-driven hazardous-material plume;
* wildfire approaching from one direction versus flooding that closes a bridge;
* a transportation accident versus a widespread infrastructure failure.

The zombie scenario may create urgency, but it does not determine the geography. Use the [FEMA National Risk Index](https://hazards.fema.gov/nri/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and local emergency-management information to identify real hazards.

For each threat, record:

1. where the danger is relative to your general area;
2. how quickly conditions could change;
3. whether officials might tell residents to evacuate or shelter in place;
4. what information would be trustworthy;
5. why the two threats require different escape directions.

# Phase 2 — Decide when to leave

An evacuation warning, watch, rumor, traffic jam, and evacuation order are not the same thing. Review [Ready.gov: Evacuation](https://www.ready.gov/evacuation){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} and [Ready.gov: Alerts and Warnings](https://www.ready.gov/alerts){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.

In **Workbook Section 2**, build a decision ladder with four levels:

| Level | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Monitor | Conditions are normal enough to remain, but official information is checked. |
| Ready | The household completes final preparation and transportation is positioned. |
| Leave voluntarily | A pre-decided evidence-based trigger makes early departure safer than waiting. |
| Leave now | Officials order evacuation or an immediate life-safety trigger occurs. |

Your triggers must be observable and specific. “It feels dangerous” is not enough. Possible evidence includes an official warning, a forecast threshold, a road or bridge closure, fire/flood movement, wind direction, transportation availability, a household medical or mobility need, or the time required to load and travel.

Then address the difficult question:

> Under what conditions should your household leave even without an order, and when could leaving early make the situation worse?

An official evacuation order overrides your voluntary threshold. If officials direct people to shelter in place—especially during a hazardous-material event—do not assume evacuation is safer.

# Phase 3 — Identify two destinations

Use **Workbook Section 3** and [Ready.gov: Make a Plan](https://www.ready.gov/plan){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.

Research one destination for each threat. They must be outside different hazard areas and reachable by the route you propose. A destination might be:

* a consenting friend or relative outside the affected area;
* a hotel or campground that could be reserved during a real event;
* a public reception center or shelter **announced for that specific event**;
* another adult-approved location with water, sanitation, and shelter.

For each destination, investigate:

* why its location is safer for that threat;
* distance and estimated travel time under normal conditions;
* whether it can accommodate everyone, including pets and accessibility needs;
* cost, hours, seasonal limits, and permission requirements;
* what happens if it is closed, full, unreachable, or inside the expanding hazard.

Do not list a school, fire station, hospital, police station, or Red Cross office as an automatic shelter. Emergency shelters are event-specific. Learn how official shelters are announced through the [American Red Cross shelter page](https://www.redcross.org/get-help/disaster-relief-and-recovery-services/find-an-open-shelter.html){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}, but do not contact or visit one for this assignment.

# Phase 4 — Build two threat-dependent routes

Create **Route A** for Threat A and **Route B** for Threat B in **Workbook Sections 4 and 5**. The routes must leave in different safe directions; Route B cannot simply rename Route A.

Use at least two mapping sources:

* a normal map for roads and distances;
* an official hazard or transportation source that could change the route.

Useful sources include:

* [Pennsylvania 511](https://www.511pa.com/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} for state-road conditions and traffic information;
* [FEMA Flood Map Service Center](https://msc.fema.gov/portal/home){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} for mapped flood hazards;
* [USGS Topographic Maps](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/national-geospatial-program/us-topo-maps-america){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"} for terrain and paper-map options;
* your county or municipality’s official emergency-management page.

For **each** route, include:

1. general starting area and destination area;
2. direction of travel;
3. primary roads, turns, bridges, tunnels, steep grades, waterways, rail crossings, or other important features;
4. total distance;
5. at least **two researched navigation checkpoints** recognizable without a phone;
6. at least **one decision checkpoint** where the household reassesses the threat, route, people, transportation, and destination;
7. one likely congestion or closure point;
8. one route-specific hazard;
9. a turnaround or alternate action if the route fails;
10. a paper map, hand-drawn map, or printed directions usable without cell service.

A checkpoint is not merely “the gas station.” Record what makes it identifiable, approximately how far it is from the previous point, what decision occurs there, and which source confirms it.

# Phase 5 — Compare transportation modes

In **Workbook Section 6**, compare every mode the entire household could realistically use:

## Vehicle

Consider licensed drivers, seats, child restraints, accessibility, fuel already available, range, traffic, road closures, parking, weather, and what happens if the vehicle fails. Do not count on refueling during a mass evacuation.

## Bicycle

Consider whether a safe working bicycle, helmet, carrying capacity, fitness, route, grade, weather, daylight, traffic exposure, and ability to keep the group together fit **every person**. A bicycle option that abandons a household member is not a household plan.

## Foot

Consider realistic group pace, distance, mobility, footwear, weather, daylight, rest, water weight, road safety, and the slowest traveler. Do not plan to walk on an interstate or other prohibited roadway.

## Public or assisted transportation

Investigate whether local authorities provide evacuation assistance, accessible transportation, or pickup instructions. Service may be event-specific. Never assume a normal bus schedule will continue.

Choose a primary mode and at least one fallback. Explain the trigger for switching modes.

# Phase 6 — Calculate whether the movement works

Use **Workbook Section 7** and show your arithmetic.

> **Travel time = moving time + congestion delay + rest/loading time**

> **Vehicle range = usable fuel × realistic distance per unit of fuel**

> **Carried load per traveler = total carried weight ÷ capable carriers**

Use researched or clearly labeled estimated speeds. Apply a congestion delay to vehicle travel and rest time to walking or cycling. Do not claim precision your evidence cannot support.

Test at least two failures:

* departure is delayed;
* the primary road closes;
* fuel cannot be purchased;
* weather changes;
* one traveler cannot maintain the planned pace;
* the destination becomes unavailable;
* the group reaches a decision checkpoint and the threat has shifted.

# Phase 7 — Decide what leaves with you

In **Workbook Section 8**, classify items by function and priority rather than copying a generic survival list.

Every selected item must answer:

1. What problem does it solve?
2. Who needs it?
3. How much does it weigh or how much space does it use?
4. Is it essential, useful, replaceable, or a comfort?
5. Who carries it?
6. What is left behind so it can come?

Consider water, ready-to-eat food, needed health items managed by an adult, first aid, clothing, weather protection, hygiene, infant or pet supplies, identification copies managed by an adult, cash, chargers, radio, paper maps, contact information, and one small morale item.

Pack for the transportation fallback, not only the vehicle. Do not submit photographs of private documents, medication labels, valuables, keys, or security equipment.

## Procurement along the way

During a mass evacuation, stores may close, electronic payment may fail, fuel may run out, and public supplies may be reserved for immediate needs. Separate:

* **carried essentials** that the plan cannot survive without;
* **verified resupply possibilities** that may be available;
* **unsafe assumptions**, such as “we can always buy water later.”

Taking supplies without permission is not procurement. Looting, trespassing, entering closed buildings, or taking emergency caches is never part of the plan.

# Phase 8 — Prepare the home without delaying departure

In **Workbook Section 9**, create a short departure checklist. Review [Ready.gov: Evacuation](https://www.ready.gov/evacuation){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.

Consider:

* official instructions about utilities—never improvise;
* doors and windows;
* pets and carriers;
* refrigerated food and power-loss concerns;
* a note or check-in to an approved contact without advertising that the home is empty;
* taking essential documents without exposing them in schoolwork;
* moving only items officials recommend protecting from the specific hazard;
* a final people-and-pets count.

Separate actions into **must do**, **do if time permits**, and **skip if delay becomes dangerous**. No house is worth missing the safe departure window.

# Phase 9 — Communicate without phone navigation

Complete **Workbook Section 10**.

Create a PACE communication plan—Primary, Alternate, Contingency, Emergency—and a lost-contact rule. Use an out-of-area contact when appropriate. Review the [Ready.gov Family Emergency Communication Plan](https://www.ready.gov/plan-form){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.

Your offline navigation package must include:

* paper directions or a paper map for both routes;
* checkpoint names and approximate distances;
* destination information held privately by an adult;
* an agreed meeting action if the group becomes separated;
* the rule for changing routes;
* a way to receive official information, such as battery radio or [NOAA Weather Radio](https://www.weather.gov/nwr/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer"}.

Do not rely on a saved phone map as your only navigation method. Batteries break; checkpoints and paper do not require a signal.

# Phase 10 — Make the recommendation

In **Workbook Section 11**, bring the plan together:

* your recommended departure threshold;
* the strongest argument for waiting;
* the strongest argument for leaving early;
* Route A and its threat;
* Route B and its threat;
* primary and fallback transportation;
* the most fragile assumption;
* the first decision that must be made now.

Your conclusion may be conditional:

> We should leave voluntarily when ______ because waiting beyond ______ creates more risk than the cost of leaving early. If ______ is the threat, use Route A; if ______ is the threat, use Route B. An official order or shelter-in-place instruction overrides this plan.

# Phase 11 — Build the evacuation briefing

Use **Workbook Section 12** to plan the presentation. The supplied PowerPoint suggests a structure, but the boxes are guides—not forms. You may delete, resize, combine, or redesign them. You may use another presentation program if your teacher can open the result.

Your briefing should show:

* the decision problem and departure threshold;
* household mobility limits without private details;
* two hazards, destinations, and routes;
* readable maps with researched checkpoints;
* transportation choice and fallback;
* a prioritized load rather than an unlimited list;
* a home departure checklist;
* offline communication and navigation;
* route-failure decisions;
* a clear final recommendation;
* sources and map/image credits.

Invite household feedback; do not demand that anyone adopt or test the plan. Record one response in the workbook. If no household member is available, identify the recommendation’s weakest assumption yourself.
