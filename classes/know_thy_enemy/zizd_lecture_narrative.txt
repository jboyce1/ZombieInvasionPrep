Zombie Neuroscience Lecture Notes

A zombie is scary because it looks human but does not act fully human. It has a human body, but many of the mental systems that make a person human seem broken, missing, or disconnected.

One way to understand a zombie is to treat it like a brain puzzle. We can ask: based on what the zombie can still do, what parts of the brain might still be working? Based on what the zombie cannot do, what parts of the brain might be damaged?

A zombie can move, turn toward sound, grab, bite, and pursue people. This tells us that the basic movement systems are still partly working. However, zombies usually move badly. They are stiff, clumsy, repetitive, and not very flexible. This suggests that they may still have access to basic movement patterns, but not full planning, coordination, or judgment.

The brainstem is probably one of the most intact parts of a zombie brain. The brainstem controls basic survival functions like breathing, heart rate, reflexes, arousal, and keeping the body active. A zombie does not need to think clearly to stand, turn, or respond to simple stimuli. The brainstem helps explain how the body can still operate at a basic level.

Movement does not come from one single part of the brain. The motor cortex helps send movement commands to the body. The premotor cortex helps prepare movements in response to things in the environment. The supplementary motor area helps organize repeated movement patterns. The basal ganglia are important for habits, action selection, and automatic movement routines. The cerebellum helps with balance, timing, and coordination. The brainstem and spinal cord help carry out posture, reflexes, and basic movement.

This helps explain how a zombie can walk without really thinking. Walking is partly automatic. Once a person has learned how to walk, the brain can run much of that pattern without conscious thought. This is why a zombie might still move even if higher-level thinking is gone.

The prefrontal cortex is probably badly damaged or disconnected in a zombie. The prefrontal cortex helps with judgment, planning, impulse control, moral reasoning, social behavior, and decision-making. A zombie does not seem to stop and think. It does not ask whether something is right or wrong. It does not plan carefully or consider consequences. It acts on impulse.

This is one of the things that makes zombies disturbing. The body still moves, but the part of the mind that says “stop” is missing.

Zombies often make sounds, but they do not usually speak in normal language. This suggests that some vocal systems may still work, but the full language system is damaged. Sound is not the same thing as language.

Broca’s area is connected to speech production. It helps organize language output and turn thoughts into structured speech. If this area or nearby systems are damaged, a person may struggle to produce fluent speech. A zombie’s groans and growls suggest that it can make sound, but cannot produce organized language.

Wernicke’s area is connected to language understanding. It helps connect words and sounds to meaning. A zombie might turn toward a human voice, but that does not mean it understands the words. It may hear sound without understanding language.

Word selection requires a larger network. The brain has to connect an idea to the correct word, choose the word, and place it into a meaningful sentence. This uses parts of the frontal lobe, temporal lobe, memory systems, and language networks. A zombie may be able to express emotion through sound, but not choose words with meaning.

The amygdala is important for fear, threat detection, emotional learning, and emotional memory. It helps the brain decide that something matters emotionally. The amygdala is not where walking is stored. Motor memory and habits are more connected to the basal ganglia, cerebellum, motor cortex, and related movement systems.

However, the amygdala may help explain why a zombie reacts to certain things. A zombie may react strongly to movement, loud sounds, human voices, familiar places, or familiar people. This does not mean the zombie remembers these things in a normal human way. It may mean that some emotional tags are still attached to them.

The zombie may not know, “This is my daughter.” But it might still experience something like, “This stimulus matters.” That is not full memory. It is more like an emotional trace.

The amygdala is also important for conditioned emotional learning. This means it helps the brain learn that certain cues predict danger, reward, pain, or emotional importance. A sound, place, face, or smell can become emotionally powerful even when a person cannot fully explain why.

This gives us a better way to understand zombie recognition. A zombie might move toward a familiar house, not because it remembers living there, but because the place still carries emotional significance. It might pause near a familiar person, not because it knows the relationship, but because some emotional tag remains.

The hippocampus is connected to episodic memory. Episodic memory is memory for personal experiences. It allows a person to remember things like “I lived here,” “This is my friend,” “This happened yesterday,” or “I remember when we were together.”

A zombie probably has little or no access to episodic memory. It may show familiarity, but not true personal memory. This creates an important difference between familiarity and remembering. A zombie might recognize that something matters without knowing what it is, who it is, or why it matters.

Procedural memory is memory for how to do things. It includes habits and learned actions like walking, reaching, grabbing, climbing, turning toward a sound, or following a familiar route. Procedural memory depends heavily on systems like the basal ganglia, cerebellum, motor cortex, and related movement circuits.

A zombie may still have procedural memory even if it has lost episodic memory. This means the body may remember how to do things even when the person no longer remembers why they are doing them.

This is a key idea: the body can remember how, even when the person no longer remembers why.

Zombies are also scary because they violate social taboos. One of the strongest taboos is eating other people. Humans are social animals. We survive by cooperating, protecting each other, forming families, building communities, and following shared rules.

Taboos are not just random rules. Many taboos protect the structure of human society. Cannibalism, uncontrolled violence, and attacking one’s own group are frightening because they destroy trust. If people cannot trust each other’s bodies, intentions, or self-control, society breaks down.

A zombie reverses this system. Instead of protecting people, it consumes them. Instead of recognizing members of its group, it attacks them. Instead of following social rules, it becomes ruled by appetite.

This suggests that the zombie’s social control systems are damaged. The prefrontal cortex can no longer restrain the impulse. The emotional system may still detect people as important, but that importance has been twisted into pursuit rather than protection.

A zombie can be understood as a body with some brain systems still working and other systems badly damaged.

The zombie may still have basic survival functions, movement, reflexes, procedural memory, emotional reactions, threat detection, limited vocalization, and partial familiarity with people or places.

The zombie likely lacks judgment, moral reasoning, impulse control, complex planning, full language, word selection, episodic memory, self-awareness, and social restraint.

This is why zombies are useful for psychology. They help us ask what makes a human being human. Is it movement? Hunger? Recognition? Memory? Language? Morality? Self-control? Emotional connection?

A zombie is frightening because it forces us to imagine a person with the human body still present, but with many of the systems that create personhood damaged, disconnected, or missing.

A zombie is what a zombie does.
